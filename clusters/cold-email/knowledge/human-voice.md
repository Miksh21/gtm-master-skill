# Human Voice: Copy That Reads Like a Person Wrote It

The last pass before any outbound message ships, after the framework and the angle are right. Frameworks decide *what* the message says; this file decides whether it sounds like a colleague or like a sequence. Applies to cold email, follow-ups, LinkedIn DMs, InMails and replies.

## 1. The say-it-aloud test (whole message, not just the opener)

Read every sentence out loud. If you would not say those exact words to a peer across a desk, rewrite the sentence.

- Contractions, short sentences, plain verbs, active voice.
- Starting a sentence with "And", "But" or "So" is fine.
- More "you" than "we".
- Memo words fail the test ("leverage", "synergy", "robust", "game-changer", "empower", "innovative solution").

| Fails | Passes |
|---|---|
| "I wanted to follow up on my previous message regarding our solution." | "did this land at a bad time?" |
| "We are excited to share how our innovative solution empowers teams." | "we pull the numbers your team now copies by hand." |

## 2. Rough edges on purpose, never errors

Flawless, perfectly punctuated copy reads as machine-made. Leave a little human roughness:
- a sentence that starts lowercase
- a missing comma
- a casual fragment

**Calibrate to the reader.** The looser they write, the looser you can be. A formal writer gets almost none.
- Chat-style LinkedIn messages can be rougher than a founder-signed email.
- Names of people, companies and places are always capitalized.

**Roughness is register, not grammar.** Declension, agreement, spelling of names and every fact stay perfect, and in Czech/Slovak copy the case endings must be flawless. A misspelled name or a wrong case is not "human", it is careless.

## 3. Never let the sequence show

The prospect cannot see your cadence. Openers that narrate it ("following up", "circling back", "just checking in", "one last try", "quick question") tell them they are in an automation.

- **The test:** read only the first line of steps 2 to 5 in a row. If those lines describe the sequence instead of saying something, rewrite them.
- Open inside the substance instead: a fact about them, the point itself, or a light connector ("since you…", "btw", "also").
- Banned phrases are examples of a *move*, not an exhaustive list. A new wording of the same move is equally banned.
- If a nudge is genuinely needed, ask a concrete question they can answer, or name a date.

## 4. Mirror the reader in replies

- **Length:** one line in, one line out. A paragraph earns a paragraph.
- **Language:** anchor to their first substantive reply. A single foreign word ("gracias", "díky") does not switch the language.
- **Greeting, sign-off, emoji:** do what they do, skip what they skip.
- **Rapid back-and-forth:** keep each message as short as theirs.

## 5. Don't repeat your own shapes

- Before drafting, scan what this person already received. Don't open consecutive messages with the same move (the same praise, the same question preamble).
- If you praise, do it in a few words ("that's smart"). Never restate their setup back to them.
- **Across channels:** if an email step and a LinkedIn step ask the same question, someone on the overlapping path gets it twice. Vary it or space it out on purpose.

## 6. Write in the reader's verbs

One product fact lands differently per role. Example: a reporting tool pitched to a RevOps lead ("do you own the pipeline dashboards?") versus a CFO ("does forecast accuracy sit with your team?").

- **Drop claims the reader cannot evaluate.** An integration detail means something to the operator and nothing to the budget holder. Swap it for what they actually screen for.
- **Match the CTA to how that role buys.** Roles that buy through formal vendor comparisons respond to an invitation to include you in the next comparison, or to a small scoped pilot, rather than "book a call".
- **Match humor to the persona.** A joke that lands with engineers can fall flat with finance.
- **Ground it in real conversations.** Pull what prospects in that role actually said on calls (situation, trigger, objections) before writing. Mirror that, not what you imagine.

## 7. Claims you can defend

- **Specific and verifiable only.** Every number traces to a customer story or an approved positioning source, or it doesn't ship.
- **Honest beats impressive.** "most teams are live in two weeks" earns more trust than "live in minutes". Naming a limitation builds credibility.
- **For price-sensitive buyers, a number they can check on their own** (a public list price, a published benchmark) persuades more than any claim.

## 8. Sender integrity

The signature, the first-person story and the sending account must be one person.
- If the mailbox and the LinkedIn seat are Anna's, every message signs Anna, and every "I" story is true for Anna. A shared company story uses "we"; never tell another person's biography in the first person.
- This breaks silently when a sequence is cloned from one sender to another, so check it first on every clone.
- Check that the sender-name variable renders the same as any hardcoded signature.

## Pre-send check (add to any stress test)

1. Every sentence passes the say-it-aloud test.
2. No sequence-narrating openers, no memo words, no em or en dashes.
3. Persona: their verbs, claims they can judge, a CTA that fits how they buy.
4. Every claim has a source.
5. Sender integrity holds.
6. Some roughness present; names capitalized; grammar and cases perfect.
7. No repeated shapes across nearby steps or channels.
